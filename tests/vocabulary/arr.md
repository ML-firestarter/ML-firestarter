# ARR

## A company has 200 customers, each paying \$500 a month. What's its ARR?

- [ ] \$100,000
- [ ] \$120,000
- [x] \$1.2 million
- [ ] \$12 million

The MRR is 200 × \$500 = \$100,000, and ARR = 12 × MRR = \$1.2 million.

## Which of these count toward ARR?

- [x] Monthly subscription fees
- [ ] A one-time setup fee
- [x] A yearly contract
- [ ] Consulting billed once for a project

Only recurring revenue counts. One-time fees for setup, consulting or hardware won't come back next year.

## What does ARR tell you?

- [ ] The revenue the company earned last year
- [x] How much revenue the current subscriptions would bring in over the next twelve months if nothing changed
- [ ] The company's profit after costs
- [ ] A forecast that includes the customers it expects to win

ARR is a snapshot: no new customers, no cancellations and no price changes. It isn't revenue already earned, and it doesn't subtract any costs.

## An AI company with usage-based pricing quotes its annualized revenue run rate. How is it calculated?

- [ ] The sum of the last twelve months of revenue
- [ ] Next month's expected revenue × 12
- [ ] The number of contracts × their average length
- [x] The latest month's revenue × 12

It can move quickly in both directions, because usage isn't fixed by a contract.

## What makes ARR shrink?

- [ ] Existing customers moving to bigger plans
- [ ] A one-time fee from last year not coming back
- [x] Churn: customers leaving or paying less
- [ ] Higher hosting costs

ARR grows with new customers and expansion, and shrinks with churn. One-time fees never counted toward it, and costs aren't subtracted from it.
