# Churn

## Monthly churn is a steady 5%. About what share of customers is left after a year?

- [ ] About 95%
- [ ] About 40%
- [x] About 54%
- [ ] About 5%

Each month 95% of the customers stay, so after twelve months $0.95^{12} \approx 0.54$ of them are left. Churn compounds, so a year doesn't lose 12 × 5% = 60%.

## With a steady monthly churn of 5%, how long does the average customer stay?

- [ ] About 5 months
- [ ] About 12 months
- [x] About 20 months
- [ ] About 95 months

With a steady churn rate, the average customer stays about 1 ÷ churn: 1 ÷ 0.05 = 20 months. That lifetime is a key input to unit economics.

## What does revenue churn count that customer churn doesn't?

- [x] Customers who stay but move to a cheaper plan
- [ ] Customers who cancel
- [ ] New customers who sign up
- [ ] One-time fees

Customer churn counts people who leave. Revenue churn counts money, so it includes downgrades too.

## A service has 1,000 subscribers paying \$10 a month. In one month 30 of them cancel, and 20 others upgrade to a \$30 plan. What's its NRR for the month?

- [ ] 97%
- [x] 101%
- [ ] 103%
- [ ] 104%

The cancellations take away \$300 of the \$10,000, and the upgrades add 20 × \$20 = \$400. NRR is (10,000 − 300 + 400) ÷ 10,000 = 101%.

## What does a net revenue retention above 100% mean?

- [ ] Revenue from existing customers is shrinking
- [ ] No customers left
- [x] Expansion outweighs churn, so revenue from existing customers grows even without new sales
- [ ] More new customers joined than old ones left

NRR compares what the customers you had a year ago pay now with what they paid then. New customers aren't part of it.
