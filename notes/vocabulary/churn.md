# Churn

Churn measures who leaves. *Customer churn* is the share of customers who cancel in a period: if 5 of 100 customers leave in a month, monthly churn is 5%. *Revenue churn* counts money instead, including customers who stay but move to a cheaper plan.

Small monthly numbers add up. If 95% of customers stay each month, about 54% are left after a year ($0.95^{12} \approx 0.54$), so 5% a month loses almost half the customers in a year. With a steady churn rate, the average customer stays about 1 ÷ churn: 20 months at 5% a month. That lifetime is a key input to [unit economics](unit-economics.md).

*Net revenue retention* (NRR) puts both sides together: the revenue from the customers you had a year ago, divided by what they paid then. Above 100% means expansion outweighs churn, so revenue from existing customers grows even without new sales.

**Example:** a service starts the month with 1,000 subscribers paying \$10 and loses 30 of them, while 20 others upgrade to a \$30 plan. Customer churn is 3%. Cancellations take away \$300 of the \$10,000, but the upgrades add \$400 (20 × \$20), so revenue from existing customers grew: NRR for the month is (10,000 − 300 + 400) ÷ 10,000 = 101%.
